Two ways to do science on things
we build, not things we find.

March & Smith argue that IT research is really two disciplines wearing one coat: design science, which builds and evaluates useful artifacts, and natural science, which theorizes about and justifies why those artifacts work. Neither is the junior partner.

Salvatore T. March & Gerald F. Smith · University of Minnesota / University of Northern Iowa

The distinction the whole paper hinges on

This is not Simon's natural-vs-artificial-phenomena split. You can do natural science about an artifact (studying how a database affects users). The line is about intent: are you trying to explain reality, or improve it?

Explains what is

Natural science

Aims to understand and explain reality. Products are judged against truth — do the claims match observed facts, and predict future ones?

  • Activities: theorize, then justify
  • Product: theories
  • Method: hypothetico-deductive testing, falsification

Creates what ought to be

Design science

Aims to devise artifacts that serve human purposes. Products are judged against utility — does it work, is it an improvement?

  • Activities: build, then evaluate
  • Product: constructs, models, methods, instantiations
  • Method: construction + performance metrics

↕ the two feed each other — see "why they need each other" below ↕

What the authors ask the field to do differently

1

Stop hunting one deep theory of IT

IT artifacts are human-designed, not natural kinds. Theories may need to be grounded in theories of the people the technology affects, not in some IT-native physics.

2

Expect your results to perish

Artifacts get replaced; so does artifact-specific knowledge. A theory of how programmers used a dead language isn't a lasting contribution.

3

Classify before you build

Explicating which of the sixteen cells your work occupies stops the field from re-building and re-naming things that already exist, ungrounded and unevaluated.
